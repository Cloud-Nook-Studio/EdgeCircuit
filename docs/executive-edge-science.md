# Executive Edge: Evidence-Aware Product Strategy

## Bottom line

The credible product promise is not “raise your IQ in two minutes.” Computerized
working-memory practice reliably improves the trained task, sometimes improves
closely related tasks, and has weak or inconsistent evidence for broad transfer
to intelligence or everyday professional performance.

The strongest product position is:

> A short, premium cognitive warm-up that practices specific attention and
> working-memory operations, measures task performance honestly, and helps the
> user launch directly into one important piece of work.

This distinguishes the app through excellent interaction design, transparent
measurement, and a practical work bridge—not inflated neuroscience claims.

## What the evidence supports

### 1. Expect task-specific gains; do not promise broad cognitive transfer

- Simons and colleagues' major evidence review found extensive evidence for
  improvement on trained tasks, less evidence for closely related tasks, and
  little evidence for distant or everyday transfer.
- Melby-Lervåg, Redick, and Hulme's meta-analysis of 87 publications found
  short-term gains on verbal and visuospatial working-memory measures, but no
  convincing far-transfer gains versus treated controls.
- An n-back meta-analysis similarly concluded that much of observed transfer is
  task-specific.

**Product decision:** report “Pulse Path accuracy,” “Digit Hold span,” and
within-task trends. Never convert these into an IQ, brain age, medical score, or
claim that the user has become generally smarter.

### 2. Vary the surface while preserving the cognitive operation

The core operation should remain recognizable across a short session, while
stimuli and layouts change between sessions. This reduces rote motor learning
and keeps the user attending to shape-position bindings rather than memorizing
one permanent board.

**Product decision now implemented:** one seeded constellation remains stable
for all three rounds, then a new three-round session receives a new layout and a
new selection of nine silhouettes from a larger shape library.

This is an evidence-informed design hypothesis, not proof of real-world
transfer. It should be tested against a fixed-layout version.

### 3. Keep challenge responsive, but avoid “difficulty theater”

Adaptive working-memory training can improve engagement and trained-task
efficiency. One randomized neuroimaging study found advantages for adaptive
versus fixed training, but the broader literature does not show that adaptivity
alone guarantees transfer.

**Product decision:** adjust span gradually from accuracy, not response speed.
Use small one-step changes, avoid punishing a single error, and target a
challenging-but-credible experience rather than constant failure.

### 4. Use recall and feedback, not passive exposure

Retrieval-practice research shows that actively reconstructing learned material
can support retention better than repeated study. That literature concerns
learning material, so it should not be used to claim that recalling abstract
shapes improves general memory. It does support the interaction pattern of
present → recall → clear feedback.

**Product decision:** keep every round active, require reconstruction, and give
immediate, minimal feedback that shows exactly what happened.

### 5. Favor short, distributed use over long “grind” sessions

Distributed-practice research strongly favors spaced over massed learning
episodes. It does not establish an exact dose for this app.

**Product hypothesis to test:** one 2–4 minute session, three to five days per
week, should create a more sustainable professional ritual than long daily
sessions. Do not reward repetitive grinding.

## The executive-use loop

### Recommended 2–4 minute experience

1. **Arrive (10 seconds):** optionally rate current readiness with one tap.
2. **Prime (three rounds):** play one focused task with a stable constellation.
3. **Read the signal (10 seconds):** show task-specific accuracy and trend,
   without normative labels.
4. **Bridge to work (20 seconds):** ask, “What gets your full attention next?”
   The user writes one concrete next action.
5. **Launch:** offer a quiet 20-minute focus block or simply close the app.

The work bridge is important. Implementation-intention research shows that
specific if–then plans can improve goal attainment. The app should not imply
that the game itself caused better work performance; instead, it should help the
user convert a deliberate pause into a concrete action.

Suggested prompt:

> When I close this session, I will work on **[one action]** for **[20 minutes]**.

## Roadmap for a credible professional product

### Near term

- Preserve the new randomized constellation for the full three-round session.
- Add a one-action “Focus Launch” prompt after either game.
- Track accuracy, span, consistency, and self-rated readiness as separate
  measures.
- Explain every metric in plain language.
- Add a selective-attention or interference-control game so the product does
  not over-index on simple maintenance memory.

### Research and validation

- Compare fixed versus varied constellations with an active-control design.
- Measure trained-task improvement and a separate, closely related transfer
  task.
- Pre-register the primary outcome and analysis.
- Test whether the Focus Launch increases the probability of beginning the
  stated work task—not whether it “boosts the brain.”
- Publish null results and distinguish exploratory findings from confirmed
  effects.

## Claims language

### Defensible now

- “Practice visual sequence recall.”
- “Track your performance on focused cognitive exercises.”
- “A short cognitive warm-up before concentrated work.”
- “Difficulty responds to your task accuracy.”

### Avoid without direct app-specific trials

- “Improve executive performance.”
- “Increase intelligence or working-memory capacity.”
- “Prevent cognitive decline.”
- “Scientifically proven to make you sharper.”
- “Equivalent to exercise, sleep, meditation, or treatment.”

## Adjacent evidence that can inform—not validate—the product

- Acute physical activity has a small average beneficial association with
  cognitive-task performance, including working memory and inhibition in some
  analyses. A future product could suggest a brief walk as an optional,
  separately described readiness practice.
- Workplace mindfulness trials support stress-reduction benefits from programs
  delivered over weeks. A 10-second settling cue can improve the feel of the
  session, but it must not inherit the claims of multi-week mindfulness
  programs.
